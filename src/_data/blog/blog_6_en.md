I have turned a lot of playlists into decks, and you can tell within about twenty tracks whether a game night is going to work. The failure is almost never the people. It is the song selection, and it goes wrong in the same three ways every time.

## The mistake: picking songs you love

This is the big one, and it catches everybody including me.

A playlist you would listen to and a playlist that makes a good guessing game are different objects. Your favourite album tracks are not recognisable from four seconds. The deep cut you are proud of knowing is a dead card: one person nods, nobody guesses, the round dies.

A game playlist is built for **recognition**, not for taste. That means choruses, singles, and songs that were unavoidable at the time. You are not curating, you are picking things a room will shout over.

The test I use: could somebody hum this after four seconds of the chorus? If not, it is a bad card in a game and a fine song everywhere else.

## The mistake: one era

Everybody builds a deck from their own good years. It plays beautifully for people who were the same age at the same time, and it shuts out everybody else at the table.

A timeline game especially needs spread. If eighty cards are all from 1995 to 2005, the years bunch up and placing a card correctly becomes guesswork rather than reasoning. You want gaps people can argue about: is this before or after that.

Aim for a real spread across decades. Then make sure every guest has at least a handful of cards that are obviously theirs, because the moment somebody recognises a song nobody else does is the best moment in the game.

## The mistake: too many, or too few

For a timeline game, 80 to 120 songs is the range. Below 80 an evening runs out. Above 120 you are cutting cards for hours and the deck never gets fully played.

For [music bingo](/[lang]/music-bingo) the maths is different, because cards are drawn from a pool: 40 to 60 songs, or everybody finishes at once.

## Two things that quietly matter

**Check the years.** Streaming services report the release date of the version you are playing, not the original. A remaster puts a 1975 song in 2011, and a timeline game with wrong years is an argument with no right answer. This is the single most common thing that breaks a homemade deck, and it is why QRSong! verifies every year rather than trusting the service.

**Skip the songs with a story attached.** In a deck meant for a group, the track that means everything to two people in the room and nothing to the other eight is a slow card. Save those for a [personal deck as a gift](/[lang]/gift-box), where that is exactly the point.

## FAQ

### How many songs do I need for a music game?

80 to 120 for a timeline game, 40 to 60 for bingo. Bingo needs fewer because every card draws a different subset from the pool, and too large a pool means nobody ever fills a card.

### What makes a good song for a music guessing game?

Recognisability in about four seconds. Choruses and singles beat album tracks, and the songs that were unavoidable at the time beat the ones you personally rate. It is a different skill from making a playlist you enjoy.

### Should I mix decades?

Yes, for two reasons. A timeline game needs the years spread out or placing cards becomes guesswork, and a single-era deck excludes everyone who was not the right age at the right time.

### Why is the release year on my cards wrong?

Because streaming services usually report the year of the version you are playing rather than the original release. Remasters and reissues are the usual culprits, and a wrong year quietly breaks a timeline game.

### Can I use a playlist somebody else made?

Yes, and it is often better than your own, because you are not attached to the songs. There are ready-made playlists by decade, genre and occasion if you want to skip the selection entirely.
