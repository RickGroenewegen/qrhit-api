A music night is an easy thing to host badly. The cards do most of the work, but the half hour of setup and the first ten minutes decide whether people play three rounds or twelve. Here is what I have learned running these, including the bits that go wrong.

## Before anyone arrives

**Test the sound.** Phone speakers do not work for a group. Anything with a bluetooth speaker in it does. Connect it, play one card, set the volume where a room talking over it can still hear the track, and leave it there.

**Scan three cards yourself.** You are checking that the service is connected, that playback starts quickly, and that you know what the app does. Finding out at the table that you are signed out is a bad start.

**Decide the settings.** Song length and start point, both in the app. Fifteen seconds and a random start point is hard and good for a confident crowd. Thirty seconds from the chorus is friendly for a mixed group. Pick before, not during.

**Have the answers face down.** Sounds obvious. The number of decks I have seen spread face up on a table is not small.

## The first ten minutes

Play one round with no scoring. Nobody understands a game from an explanation, and everyone understands it after one card. Scan, let it play, let people shout, turn the card over.

Start with a card most of the room will get. Opening on something obscure sets the tone that this is a game you lose, and half the table checks out.

Explain one rule, not six. For a timeline game it is "put it where you think it goes on the line". Everything else can be answered as it comes up.

## Keeping it moving

The thing that kills a music night is dead air between cards. Somebody scrolling, somebody looking up an answer, a phone that has locked itself.

One person scans, all evening. Handing the phone around feels fairer and doubles the length of every gap. If people want to take turns, take turns at guessing.

Keep the played cards in a visible pile. In a timeline game that pile is the board, and in bingo it is how you settle a disputed win.

## When it starts to sag

Every deck has a point, usually around forty cards in, where attention drops. Two things fix it:

**Change the game.** The same deck plays [bingo](/[lang]/music-bingo), a [quiz](/[lang]/music-quiz) and a [timeline](/[lang]/music-timeline). Switching format halfway through resets the room without introducing anything new to learn.

**Shorten the snippets.** Drop to ten or fifteen seconds. Faster rounds, more of them, and the energy comes back.

## What to do about the person who knows everything

There is one at every table. Two options that both work: make them the host, which takes them out of the guessing and gives them something to do, or play in pairs and put them with somebody who does not know the music at all.

Do not handicap them. It is a party, not a tournament.

## If the deck is getting familiar

A fixed deck stops being a game once everyone has learned it. That is the moment to [build another one from a different playlist](/[lang]/generate/playlist): a different decade, a genre nobody at the table listens to, or one built from songs each guest nominated.

The free app is on [iPhone and Android](/[lang]/download-app), and it reads Hitster cards and selected other music cards too, so a deck you already own mixes in with a new one.

## FAQ

### How long should each song play in a music game?

Fifteen to thirty seconds. Fifteen is brisk and hard, thirty is comfortable. Any longer and people stop guessing and start listening, which is pleasant but is no longer a game.

### How many people can play?

It works from about three to a dozen. Above eight, play in pairs: it keeps everybody involved and stops the quickest person answering every card.

### Do I need a speaker?

Effectively yes. Phone speakers do not carry over a room of people talking, and the game depends on everyone hearing the track clearly. Any bluetooth speaker is enough.

### What do I do if someone knows every song?

Make them the host so they scan rather than guess, or play in pairs and pair them with somebody who does not know the music. Handicapping people at a party goes down badly.

### How do I keep the game from dragging?

One person scans all evening, keep the played cards visible, and shorten the snippets when attention drops. Switching to a different game with the same deck also resets the room without anyone having to learn something new.
